What is the "Standard of Perfection?"

The rulebook every Dwarf Hotot is measured against — and the reason you'll see terms like "eyeband" and "show quality" on this site.

The Standard of Perfection (SOP) is the official guidebook published by the American Rabbit Breeders Association (ARBA) describing the "ideal" version of every recognized rabbit breed. Each breed's section is written by that breed's national specialty club — for Dwarf Hotots, the American Dwarf Hotot Rabbit Club — and adopted by ARBA.

At an ARBA-sanctioned show, a licensed judge compares each rabbit to this written standard — not to the other rabbits in the room — and scores it out of 100 points. Breeders use the same standard as a roadmap for choosing breeding stock, which is why the language of the SOP shows up throughout our site.

This page is a plain-language summary for education, not a reproduction of the official text. The complete standard is published in ARBA's Standard of Perfection book, available at arba.net.

Breed Origin
1970s

Developed in Germany through two separate breeding efforts — one crossing a Ruby-Eyed-White Netherland Dwarf with a Blanc de Hotot, the other working a Netherland Dwarf line down to just the eye marking — that were eventually combined. Imported to the U.S. in the early 1980s and recognized by ARBA in 1983, earning the nickname "The Eyes of the Fancy."

General Type — Body

"Two balls" — a head and a body

Breeders often describe the ideal silhouette as two rounded shapes stacked together: a round head set on a round, compact body.

35Body

Body

Short, compact, and well rounded, with uniform width from the shoulders to the hips and well-rounded hindquarters. The topline should curve gradually from the base of the ears up to its highest point over the hips, then fall in a smooth curve down to the base of the tail.

Faulted forA long or narrow body shape that breaks from the smooth, rounded topline.
General Type — Head & Ears

A round, well-filled head

15Head

Head

Round, well filled, and set on a very short neck — proportioned to give that compact "second ball" look next to the body. The muzzle should be full, not narrow or pointed.

Faulted forA long or narrow head; a pointed or weak muzzle.
15Ears

Ears

Short, well furred, and of good substance, carried upright in a "V" shape, balanced with the size of the head and body. Ear length is measured across the top of the head from base to base.

Faulted forThin ears with little fur covering.

Disqualified for: ears measuring more than 2.75 inches in length.

Color & Markings — 20 Points Combined

The eyeband: the whole point of the breed

Everywhere else on the rabbit, the standard calls for pure white — the eyeband is the single feature the entire breed is built around, and it carries 15 points on its own.

Body color is judged separately at 5 points: the coat should be pure white over the entire animal except for the eyebands — any spot of color elsewhere on the body is a disqualification. Markings are judged at 15 points: each eyeband should form a narrow, well-defined, complete ring of colored fur fully outlining the eye, roughly the thickness of two stacked pennies wide, even all the way around. An eyeband that's irregular, overly wide, or fades ("feathers") into the surrounding white fur is faulted; an incomplete band or heavy feathering is a disqualification.

Condition — 5 Points

Health and presentation

"Condition" refers to the rabbit's overall health, grooming, and presentation at the moment it's judged — clean fur, no stains, good flesh condition, and freedom from anything suggesting poor health or care.

A rabbit can be a wonderful, healthy, loving pet without meeting every point of the written standard. We breed toward the standard because it reflects decades of accumulated knowledge about correct, healthy structure in this breed — but every rabbit we raise, show-quality or not, is raised with the same care.
