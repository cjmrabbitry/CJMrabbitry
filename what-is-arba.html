What Is ARBA,
and Why It Matters

Every serious rabbit breeder eventually runs into these four letters. Here's what the American Rabbit Breeders Association actually is, what it does, and why it quietly underpins nearly everything of value in the rabbit world — from the pedigree on your doe to the breed itself surviving another generation.

Rabbitry Management Center Education 12 min read

Key Takeaways

  • ARBA is the national governing body for domestic rabbits and cavies in North America — it sets breed standards, licenses judges, and certifies pedigrees.
  • Its roots go back to 1910; it has operated continuously under evolving names for over a century.
  • The Standard of Perfection is the rulebook breeders are judged against — currently covering more than 50 recognized rabbit breeds and 13 cavy breeds.
  • Registration and judging aren't rubber stamps — both require years of experience, testing, and peer endorsement.
  • For breeders, ARBA membership converts a hobby into something verifiable: documented lineage, credible pricing, and a real community.

Where It Started

ARBA didn't begin as a rabbit organization at all — it began as a response to a craze.

In the late 1800s, a domestic rabbit breed called the Belgian Hare swept the country in a wave of popularity. By 1910, that enthusiasm had organized itself into a formal club: the National Pet Stock Association. Over the next fifteen years the group changed names and headquarters several times — reflecting a period when the organization actually promoted far more than rabbits, including cavies, foxes, raccoons, and even rats and mice. By 1925 the group had narrowed its focus and become the American Rabbit and Cavy Breeders Association, setting the trajectory that would eventually become the ARBA known today.

That century-plus of continuous operation matters more than it might seem. Livestock organizations that survive that long tend to do so because breeders keep needing the thing they provide: a shared, trusted standard everyone agrees to be judged against.

What ARBA Actually Does

At its core, ARBA exists to promote, develop, and improve the domestic rabbit and cavy — across every use case, from a single house pet to a several-hundred-head commercial operation.

It's a non-profit, membership-run organization with roughly 20,000 members across the United States, Canada, and abroad. That membership base is deliberately wide: pet owners, 4-H and FFA youth, hobby breeders, show exhibitors, and commercial producers all sit under the same organization, working from the same rulebook.

Practically, that mission plays out through a handful of concrete functions: writing and maintaining official breed standards, licensing the judges and registrars who apply them, sanctioning shows, running an annual national convention, and operating a youth program with its own contests and scholarships. None of it is ornamental — each piece solves a real problem breeders would otherwise face alone.

The Standard of Perfection

If ARBA has a single founding document, it's this one.

Published roughly every five years, the Standard of Perfection is the book that defines what an ideal example of each recognized breed looks like — body type, fur, color, weight, condition — along with the disqualifications that pull an animal out of competition entirely. It also lays out the exact process a new breed or variety has to go through to earn official recognition, which is deliberately slow and rigorous by design.

This is the part that's easy to undersell: without an agreed written standard, "purebred" and "show quality" are just marketing words. With one, they mean something specific and checkable — the same Holland Lop being evaluated in Maryland and one being evaluated in Texas are being held to the identical printed description.

Registration: Proving the Pedigree

Registration is where ARBA turns a rabbit's history into something documented.

A licensed ARBA registrar examines an animal against its breed standard and its pedigree — verifying that its lineage checks out for at least three generations — before the rabbit can carry an official ARBA registration number. That number becomes part of the animal's permanent record and travels with it if it's sold.

Becoming a registrar isn't casual, either. Candidates need years of continuous membership, written endorsements from twenty current ARBA members, and a passing score on both a written and oral exam administered by a licensed judge. That bar exists on purpose — a registration is only worth something if the person granting it has genuinely earned the authority to.

Judges: The Standard, in Practice

Judges are where the written standard meets the actual rabbit on the table. Earning a judge's license is a multi-year process layered on top of registrar experience: at minimum, five years raising and showing rabbits or cavies, two of those years as a licensed registrar, dozens of animals personally registered, and endorsements from twenty ARBA members in good standing. From there, candidates sit extensive written and oral exams and must assist in judging at least eight shows under six different licensed judges — each of whom has to sign off.

Licensing isn't the finish line, either. Working judges are required to attend a judges' conference at least once every five years and pass a yearly review exam with a minimum score of 80% just to keep their license active. That ongoing accountability is a big part of why an ARBA placing carries weight — the person awarding it answers to the same organization the breeder does.

Shows and the National Convention

Local and regional shows run under ARBA sanction throughout the year, giving breeders in every district a place to compete, network, and get animals evaluated by licensed judges. All of it builds toward the ARBA National Convention — a week-long annual event that draws thousands of rabbits and cavies along with the breeders, judges, and youth members who bring them, for the largest competitions, educational contests, and Hall of Fame recognitions the organization holds.

For a breeder, showing under ARBA sanction is what turns "I think this is a nice rabbit" into a documented placing that other breeders and buyers actually recognize.

The Youth Program

ARBA's youth division isn't an afterthought — it's one of the more structured 4-H-adjacent programs in American livestock. Young members compete in judging contests, breed identification, quiz bowl, showmanship, and Youth Royalty competitions, most of it culminating at the National Convention alongside the adult show. Youth members who've held membership for three or more years become eligible to apply for ARBA educational scholarships in their graduating year — a real, tangible payoff for kids who grow up inside the organization.

Why This Matters to Every Breeder

Strip away the acronym and ARBA is solving a problem every livestock community eventually runs into: how do you make quality, lineage, and reputation mean the same thing to a stranger that they mean to you?

Trust

Verified Lineage

A registered rabbit's pedigree has been checked by a licensed third party — not just claimed by the seller. That's the difference between "purebred" as a promise and as a fact.

Trust

A Shared Standard

The Standard of Perfection means "show quality" is a specific, printed, checkable description — not a subjective sales pitch that varies rabbitry to rabbitry.

Preservation

Breeds That Would Otherwise Vanish

Rare breeds survive because a standard exists to breed toward and a community exists that cares whether they're maintained at all.

Preservation

A Real Path to Expertise

The registrar-to-judge pipeline turns experienced breeders into the next generation of evaluators, keeping the standard alive rather than static on a page.

Community

Nearly 20,000 Members

From single-pet owners to commercial operations, all working from the same rulebook — a genuinely rare thing to have in a hobby this dispersed.

Community

A Future for Youth

Structured contests and scholarships give the next generation of breeders a reason to stay — and a credential to show for it.

ARBA isn't the reason people fall in love with rabbits. But it's a large part of why that love turns into something durable — breeds worth preserving, pedigrees worth trusting, and a community that outlasts any single rabbitry, including this one.
